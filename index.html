Theory behind the numbers

    
Every readout above opens to show its formula with your values substituted. These notes explain why each choice moves the result the way it does.

    

      
1 · Field from amp-turns
        
Field strength scales with the product N·I (amp-turns). A long solenoid has a nearly uniform interior field; outside it, and in front of a flat spiral, the field falls off quickly once you are farther away than about one coil radius.

        
B_center = μ₀·N·I / √(ℓ² + D²)
B_axis(z) = Σ μ₀·I·a² / (2·(a² + z²)^{3/2})   (sum over turns)

        
This bench models each coil as rings of current and sums the exact ring field (elliptic integrals) everywhere in the map. The practical rule: to reach deeper tissue, make the coil wider, not just stronger. Field at depth z drops roughly as 1/z³ once z exceeds the coil radius.

      

      
2 · Inductance, resistance and the time constant
        
Inductance grows with N², so doubling turns doubles field per amp but quadruples L. With a fixed supply voltage, a bigger L means current rises more slowly (τ = L/R) and, for AC, impedance ωL limits current. Above a crossover frequency f ≈ R/(2πL), adding turns stops helping and starts hurting.

        
L (µH) ≈ 0.8·a²·N² / (6a + 9b + 10c)     Wheeler multilayer, inches
τ = L / R_total        X_L = 2π·f·L        Q = ωL / R_ac

        
Thicker wire (lower AWG number) lowers R and heat, but takes more space, so the winding gets deeper and the mean turn longer.

      

      
3 · Faraday induction in tissue
        
Tissue responds to the electric field a changing B induces, not to B itself. For a circular path of radius r centred on the axis, the induced field is E = (r/2)·dB/dt and the current density is J = σ·E. Fast edges matter more than peak field: a 1 mT pulse that rises in 50 µs gives 20 T/s, far more induced E than a 10 mT field rising over 10 ms.

        
E = (r/2)·dB/dt        J = σ·E        SAR = σ·E_rms² / ρ

        
Nerve excitation follows a strength–duration curve: E_th = E_rheo·(1 + τ_c/t_phase). The bench uses E_rheo ≈ 6 V/m and τ_c ≈ 150 µs, an order-of-magnitude figure for large myelinated peripheral fibres. Above about 100 kHz, stimulation gives way to heating (SAR) as the limiting effect.

      

      
4 · Frequency regimes, low to high
        
    
          
  • ELF PEMF (1–100 Hz): pulse or square drive, mT-range fields, ms-scale pulses. Edges set dB/dt. Large-diameter flat spirals or Helmholtz pairs.
    • 
          
  • Pulsed bursts (kHz carrier, Hz repetition): a kHz carrier gated in bursts packs many fast edges into a low repetition rate, the pattern used by burst-type bone-growth stimulators.
    • 
          
  • Eddy-current induction (10 kHz–1 MHz): series-resonant tank, litz wire, ferrite. Induced current density and heating rise with f.
    • 
          
  • MIT (100 kHz–10 MHz): excitation plus receiver coil. Tissue conductivity produces a tiny secondary field, 90° out of phase, so the measurement is a phase shift of 1–100 millidegrees.
    • 
        

      

      
5 · Skin and proximity effect in the winding
        
At frequency f, current crowds into a copper layer of depth δ = √(ρ/(π·f·μ₀)), about 2 mm at 1 kHz and 66 µm at 1 MHz. In multilayer windings the neighbouring layers make it far worse (proximity effect). Dowell's formula gives the AC/DC resistance ratio F_R from layer count and wire-to-skin-depth ratio.

        
ξ = (d/δ)·(√π/2)·√η
F_R = ξ·[ (sinh2ξ + sin2ξ)/(cosh2ξ − cos2ξ) + (2(p²−1)/3)·(sinhξ − sinξ)/(coshξ + cosξ) ]

        
When F_R climbs past ~2, use litz wire, fewer layers, or a single-layer spaced winding.

      

      
6 · Cores: permeability, saturation, loss
        
A magnetic core multiplies flux, but an open rod only delivers a fraction of its material permeability because of demagnetisation: μ_rod = μ / (1 + N_d·(μ − 1)), with N_d set by the length-to-diameter ratio. Long thin rods win.

        
Every core has a saturation limit (≈1.5 T for iron, ≈0.4 T for MnZn ferrite) beyond which extra current adds almost nothing, and a frequency limit beyond which eddy and hysteresis losses dominate. Solid iron is only good near DC; MnZn ferrite to a few hundred kHz; NiZn ferrite and RF iron powder into the MHz range. A ferrite plate behind a flat spiral roughly doubles field on the open side.

      

      
7 · Resonance and self-resonance
        
A series capacitor C = 1/(ω²L) cancels the coil's reactance at the drive frequency, so the driver only sees R and the current rises by a factor of Q. The capacitor then carries Q times the drive voltage, so its voltage rating matters.

        
Every coil also has parasitic turn-to-turn and layer-to-layer capacitance. At its self-resonant frequency (SRF) it stops acting as an inductor. Keep the operating frequency below about a third of SRF. Multilayer windings with many turns have the lowest SRF.

      

      
8 · ESP32-S3 + Si5351 drive architecture
        
    
          
  • The Si5351 makes clean 50 % square waves from about 2.3 kHz to 200 MHz on three outputs, set over I²C. It cannot make low frequencies, variable duty, or sine waves directly.
    • 
          
  • The ESP32-S3 makes low-frequency timing (LEDC / MCPWM) and gating envelopes. It has no DAC, so analogue sine below the Si5351 range needs an external DDS (AD9833) or DAC plus a linear amplifier.
    • 
          
  • Below the Si5351 range the ESP32-S3 does the timing itself: an LEDC/GPIO output can go straight to the gate of a logic-level MOSFET (series gate resistor, 10 kΩ pull-down, flyback diode or TVS across the coil). The pin only charges the gate; the coil current flows through the FET. With ≈40 mA of gate current, edges take Q_g/I_g, usually well under a microsecond, which is fast next to typical PEMF coil time constants. A gate driver becomes worthwhile for high-Q_g parts, tens of kHz, or when you want the sharpest edges for dB/dt.
  • Bipolar drive needs an H-bridge (e.g. DRV8871 with two GPIOs); a single low-side FET gives unipolar pulses.
    • 
          
  • Burst mode: Si5351 carrier AND-gated by an ESP32 envelope (74LVC1G08), so I²C is never in the timing path.
    • 
          
  • Feedback: a shunt plus INA240 for current, a linear Hall sensor (DRV5055, DC–20 kHz) or a pickup coil for field, AD8302 gain/phase detector for MIT.
    • 
        

      

      
9 · MIT signal size
        
A conducting sphere of radius a and conductivity σ in a field B₀ develops eddy currents with magnetic moment m = ω·σ·B₀·2πa⁵/15, in quadrature with the primary field. Seen from a receiver at distance d, the ratio of secondary to primary field is the phase shift the system must resolve.

        
ΔB/B ≈ μ₀·ω·σ·a⁵ / (15·d³) · (B₀ / B_rx)        Δφ ≈ ΔB/B (rad)

        
Signal grows linearly with frequency and conductivity, and very steeply with sample size. Tissue skin depth at 10 MHz is still ~20 cm, so the low-frequency approximation holds for most samples.

      

      
10 · Exposure references and safety
        
The bench compares field at the target with the ICNIRP 2010 general-public reference levels (1 Hz–10 MHz). These are conservative screening values for unintended exposure, not therapeutic limits. Exceeding them is a signal to treat the build as a medical-device question with clinical oversight.

        
Do not use near pacemakers, cochlear implants, neurostimulators, insulin pumps or metallic implants. Watch winding temperature on skin contact. High-voltage resonant capacitors and inductive turn-off spikes can injure you and destroy MOSFETs.
